***Eight - Three - The Wisest of Numbers***

*I set out to write eight encounters this week, but only wrote three. You know, "that's life," and May is just around the corner.*

I've been reading Ursula K. Le Guin's non-fiction works lately, they make me feel at home. I'm not sure why; there's just something about her conversational style. She feels like an old friend.
Today I also learned that both the idea behind Bram Stoker's Dracula, aside from folklore and Carmilla, and the concept of "school of magic" are based on an article Emily Gerard wrote after living in Transylvania for two years collecting folk beliefs.
It's called Transylvanian Superstitions. If you are interested, you should also check out her book; it's available on archive.org: The Land Beyond the Forest; Facts, Figures, and Fancies From Transylvania.

Ursula's direct opinions on S&S literature, indirect ones on Robert E. Howard, and (I guess direct?) thoughts on Lovecraft and his readers made me really sad. Her way of designing worlds, by not designing but discovering them apparently (Dreams Must Explain Themselves) was really inspiring.
I appreciated her rule about "changing four words to completely change the setting," because I checked some of our works and it doesn't apply to our writing.

In From Elfland to Poughkeepsie, she argues that if you can alter a narrative on such a massive scale, teleporting the story from the Middle Ages to 1973 by changing only four words, you've made the crucial mistake of selling your artistic soul for accessibility.
We've discussed accessibility at length with the team, quite a few times at this point. It is true that reading about "bakters," "never-alls," "homins," and "Makrofaj soldiers with names like Kellan" is alienating and a bit hard to connect with for the players.
However, that's simply the cost of fantasy; it's alien, and it's beautiful. It was reassuring to see an author appreciating that alienation the way we do. After all, we are writing fantasy (and some sci-fi too, though you haven't seen that yet), and we will probably always be writing speculative fiction.
So, it is only natural for it to be "fantastique."

The blood is indistinguishable from the mud. I can’t tell where the trail begins or ends. Perhaps the crash happened when the cart tipped over. The splintered side of the cart is a chaotic mess. Were these swords swung in panic, or the jagged, tearing work of something with claws? No clear picture forms in my mind. The noises from the sick man hang in the air like a deeply unsettling melody.

The only thing I know for sure is that this forest is not safe.

Just as I'm about to turn back, a pungent stench hits the air, sparking a flash in my mind. The forest around me fades. I look up; the interwoven leaves and the blue sky give way to a dense, shifting darkness... The vision that bleeds back in is... similar. I am still in a forest, but undeniably a different one. I’m crouched in a thicket. My hands are slick with a warm, sticky fluid. In front of me lies a massive snare. I pour the fluid dead center into the trap. Turning to the shadow beside me, I whisper: "Ready."

The stench begins to throb in my mind with a heavy rhythm, like a grim march. My head spins. I can't stop the wave of acid rising from my stomach to my throat... My temples pound, stealing my breath. I vomit.

The sound of my own retching, the sick man’s screams, and the dead silence of the forest meld together into a profound sense of dread.

My knees buckle, and I collapse onto the wet earth. Despite my empty stomach, bile pours from my mouth in torrents. I must be sick. As my clothes become caked in mud, purging this poison eventually brings an incredible sense of relief. I needed this.

"Stop," says a gruff voice.

Footsteps approach. Heavy, cautious strides squelching through the mud. The blond one raises his bow. Just as he is about to draw the string, the other reaches out and grabs his wrist.

"Hell, it's just a kid."

The bow slowly lowers. The man’s gaze stays locked on me. The lines of his face are set in stone, but the savage edge in his voice has softened:

"Are you alright?"

Without waiting for an answer, he adds:

"Come here. The Bakters... they've gone crazy."
